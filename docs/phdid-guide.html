The problem this package solves

Why a staggered DiD design leaves you with a specification question after the bias question is settled.

In a staggered design, units enter treatment at different times, so the treatment effect is not a number. It is a vector of cohort-time effects — one CATT per cohort-time cell, K of them.

The modern literature has largely settled how to estimate that vector without bias. What it has said much less about is what to do next. Estimating all K effects separately is unbiased, but wastes degrees of freedom whenever some of those effects are genuinely equal, and reports intervals wider than they need to be. Collapsing them into a single pooled TWFE coefficient is precise, but biased for every individual effect the moment the heterogeneity is real — and a single number cannot describe a heterogeneous response whatever weighted average it happens to target.

Between those corners sits partial homogeneity: a partition of the K cells into m < K groups, within which the effects are equal. Under the true partition the grouped estimator is unbiased and strictly more efficient than the flexible one — in a balanced orthogonal design, exactly |C| times more efficient for every cell in a group of size |C|. The partition is of course unknown. Recovering it is what this package does.

These are not rival procedures. Holding the error variance fixed and adopting a pairwise prior on the partition, the ℓ0 estimator is the maximum a posteriori partition of the same Bayesian model the sampler explores; the two differ only in the partition prior. Report l0_ph() when you want a grouping a reader can hold in their head, and bayes_ph() when you want honest uncertainty.

The one-line summary

Use homogeneity_test() to decide whether to group at all, l0_ph() to say how, and bayes_ph() to say how sure.

When the method delivers, and when it does not

The gains are real but conditional. Everything turns on separation — the distance between distinct effect levels measured in standard errors of the flexible estimates, written δ throughout. At δ = 6 both estimators cut the sampling variance of the cohort-time effects by 26–52% relative to flexible TWFE at essentially no bias cost. At δ = 12 they approach the infeasible oracle. At δ = 3 the partition cannot be recovered reliably, selection noise erases the advantage, and both feasible estimators end up less precise than simply estimating everything separately.

The methods deliver when heterogeneity is clumpy — a modest number of well-separated levels. They should not be expected to when the effects form a near-continuum, and the package is built to say so out loud rather than let a tidy-looking partition speak for itself.

Installation

One dependency-light install; did is optional and only needed for one entry point.

# install.packages("remotes")
remotes::install_github("ronwag2005/phdid")

The package imports only stats, utils, graphics and grDevices — all base R. did, testthat, knitr and rmarkdown are suggested: did is required only if you want to hand an att_gt() fit directly to ph_data(), and the others only for tests and vignettes. There is no compiled code, so no toolchain is needed.

The workflow

Five steps in a genuine order — each one depends on the answer to the last.

  1. Estimate the cohort-time effects somewhere else

    phdid does not estimate CATTs. It takes them from whichever heterogeneity-robust estimator suits your design, together with their joint sampling covariance.

    library(phdid); library(did)
data(mpdta, package = "did")

first <- att_gt(yname = "lemp", tname = "year", idname = "countyreal",
                gname = "first.treat", control_group = "notyettreated",
                data = mpdta, bstrap = FALSE)

  2. Assemble

    ph_data() keeps the post-treatment cells and takes the exact joint covariance from the estimator's influence functions.

    d <- ph_data(first)

  3. Ask whether there is anything to recover

    Before grouping. Both estimators will partition pure noise if you ask them to.

    homogeneity_test(d)
#> chi-squared = 25.29 on 6 df,  p = 0.000302
#> heterogeneity share       : 80%
#> Reading: recoverable heterogeneity.

  4. Recover a partition

    l0_ph(d)
#> Groups (m) : 4   (selected by BIC, n = 7 in the penalty)
#> Mean variance ratio among pooled cells: 0.50

  5. Do inference that admits the partition is uncertain

    fit <- bayes_ph(d, alpha = 1, iters = 20000, burn = 2000)
aggregate(fit, "overall")
#>     term estimate std.error conf.low  conf.high
#>  overall -0.02405   0.01199 -0.04763 -0.0006204

coclustering(fit)   # which groupings are firm

Assembling data

One constructor, three entry points, and the theorem that makes them interchangeable.

ph_data()

Every estimator in the package operates on the same two objects: a vector τ̂ of first-stage cohort-time effects and their joint sampling covariance Σ̂. This is not a convenience. It is a sufficiency result — the collapsed marginal likelihood and the group-effect posterior depend on the data only through R'Σ̂⁻¹τ̂ and R'Σ̂⁻¹R, where R is the group-indicator matrix. Any consistent first stage may therefore be used, and nothing downstream depends on it beyond that pair.

From a did::att_gt() fit

ph_data(x, weights = c("cohort", "equal"), ...)

Keeps post-treatment cells (t ≥ g) and takes Σ̂ = V_analytical / n. Fit with bstrap = FALSE so the analytical covariance is computed. Default weights are cohort sizes, giving a population-weighted overall ATT; reconstructed standard errors are checked against those did reports and a mismatch warns.

From estimates you already have

This is the route for fixest interactions, stacked event studies, imputation estimators, or any first stage you ran yourself.

From a micro panel

ph_data(x, yname, idname, tname, gname, xformla = NULL, weights = c("cohort", "equal"), ...)

Fits the fully flexible (interacted) TWFE model internally and returns τ̂ with σ̂²(D̃'D̃)⁻¹. Unit and time fixed effects — and any covariates in xformla — are partialled out by alternating projections, which handles unbalanced panels correctly. By Frisch–Waugh–Lovell, covariate adjustment leaves the partition problem unchanged.

Never-treated units may be coded 0, Inf or NA. This route assumes spherical errors, since it estimates a single σ̂²; for clustered or serially correlated errors, run a first stage that reports a robust joint covariance and use the pair route instead. It refuses panels needing a dense design matrix above 5×10⁷ elements and tells you to use the two-stage route instead.

The covariance is not diagonal

Cohort-time cells share units and share the unit and time fixed effects, so their estimates are correlated. Using the exact joint covariance is what makes the reported intervals honest: treating cells as independent understates the posterior variance of aggregates such as the overall ATT by a factor of about 3.5 in the paper's design.

On the mpdta data, covariance_check() finds the diagonal shortcut moves individual co-clustering probabilities by up to 0.70 and shifts the posterior expected number of groups from 2.20 to 2.64. ph_data() warns when handed a diagonal covariance so that the choice is at least deliberate.

Estimators

The two corners, the fit under a known partition, and the two recovery procedures.

ph_fit() — the grouped model under a given partition

ph_fit(object, partition)

The workhorse everything else calls. Given a partition with indicator matrix R, it solves the restricted GLS problem

phi_hat = (R' Sigma^-1 R)^-1 R' Sigma^-1 tau_hat
Var(phi_hat) = (R' Sigma^-1 R)^-1

and assigns each cell its group effect. partition is an integer vector of length K (labels need not be consecutive) or a list of index vectors. Hand it the true partition and you have the infeasible oracle estimator.

Why not just average within groups

The obvious shortcut — averaging the flexible estimates within each group — coincides with this fit only when the within-transformed dummies are orthogonal. In a general panel, cells sharing a cohort share unit fixed effects and cells in the same period share time fixed effects, so R'Σ̂⁻¹R has non-zero off-diagonals, the group means fail the normal equations, and they are no longer best linear unbiased. This is why l0_ph() re-estimates after its search rather than reporting merged means.

flex_twfe() and pooled_twfe() — the benchmarks

flex_twfe(object) pooled_twfe(object)

The two corners of the partition problem, provided so every comparison runs through the same code path as the estimators being compared.

flex_twfe() is the partition into K singletons; it returns the first-stage estimates and covariance unchanged, which makes it a useful identity check. pooled_twfe() imposes one common coefficient on all cells. Because the grouped regressor is the sum of the clean cohort-time dummies, this is the pooled TWFE coefficient — the most precise estimator available, and biased for every individual effect under heterogeneity. Its intervals are short and centred on the wrong estimand, which is why the paper's coverage table reports 0.04 for it.

l0_ph() — the ℓ0-penalised estimator

l0_ph(object, select = c("bic", "lambda", "m"), lambda = NULL, m = NULL, search = c("greedy", "exact-cost"), n_bic = NULL)

Recovers a partition by minimising RSS(P) + lambda * c(P), where c(P) counts pairs of cells placed in different groups. The ℓ0 form is used rather than an ℓ1 fusion penalty because it produces exact equality of coefficients rather than continuous shrinkage.

The two steps

Search. A greedy agglomerative pass from K singletons, merging at each stage the pair minimising n_A n_B / (n_A + n_B) * (tau_A - tau_B)^2 - lambda |A||B|. Runs in O(K³).

Re-estimation. The grouped model is re-fitted by GLS under the selected partition. This is not a refinement — the merge criterion treats the design as orthogonal, and only the re-estimated fit is best linear unbiased in a general panel.

ArgumentMeaning
select"bic" sweeps the whole path and minimises BIC, needing no tuning grid. "lambda" applies the stopping rule at a supplied penalty. "m" takes a fixed number of groups
lambdathe penalty, on the threshold scale: two singleton cells merge when their squared difference, weighted by the harmonic mean of their effective sample sizes, falls below it
search"greedy" is the published algorithm. "exact-cost" is an extension: it scores each candidate merge by its true GLS deviance increment under the full covariance, removing the orthogonality approximation from the search. Still greedy, so still a heuristic. Costs O(K⁵) — small K only
n_bicsample size in the BIC penalty on the two-stage route. Default K. This changes your answer

Why BIC works without tuning: a correct merge costs O_p(sigma^2) while a wrong merge costs O(N * delta_tau^2), so any threshold between the two separates them, and BIC's effective threshold sits in that window.

bayes_ph() — the Dirichlet Process estimator

bayes_ph(object, alpha = 1, mu0 = NULL, sigma0_sq = NULL, iters = 4000L, burn = 1000L, thin = 1L, chains = 1L, sigma2 = c("auto", "random", "fixed"), marginal = c("exact", "diagonal"), init = "dispersed", a0 = 0.001, b0 = 0.001, seed = NULL, progress = interactive())

Places a Dirichlet Process mixture prior on the cohort-time effects and samples the posterior with a collapsed Gibbs sampler. Because draws from a DP are almost surely discrete, realisations put positive probability on ties among the effects — and those ties are the partition. Every partition gets positive probability; parsimonious ones are favoured; their number is never fixed.

ArgumentMeaning
alphaconcentration. Controls the prior expected number of groups through E[m] ≈ alpha * log(1 + K/alpha). Small favours pooling, large favours the flexible fit
mu0, sigma0_sqGaussian base measure. NULL uses data-scaled defaults: median(tau) and (10 * sd(tau))^2
iters, burn, thinsweeps per chain, burn-in discarded, thinning interval
chainsmore than one enables ph_rhat(); chains start from dispersed partitions
sigma2"auto" draws the error variance each sweep when the object carries a micro panel and conditions on the supplied covariance otherwise
marginal"exact" evaluates the full collapsed marginal likelihood at every assignment move, so the moves see the cross-cell covariance. "diagonal" uses the fast shortcut that ignores it
init"dispersed", "singletons", "pooled", "random", or an explicit label vector
a0, b0inverse-gamma prior on the error variance; used only when it is drawn

On the error variance

When the object came from a micro panel, σ² is a real parameter and is drawn from its inverse-gamma full conditional each sweep. When it came from a first-stage estimator, the covariance is a fixed plug-in and there is no scalar variance left to sample, so the sampler conditions on it. Appendix D of the paper shows the two treatments give the same coverage and interval length to two decimals, so nothing hinges on this.

Setting the concentration

alpha_for_groups(K, target) # solve E[m] for alpha expected_groups(K, alpha) # the forward direction
alpha_for_groups(18, 9)   #> 7.0  (the paper's simulation setting)
expected_groups(7, 1)     #> 2.08 (the applications' alpha = 1)
How much alpha matters

Not much for aggregates in a large design; potentially a lot when K is small. The paper's solution paths are nearly flat in alpha for the overall ATT in its simulation, but its seven-cell application sees the overall effect slide from about −0.017 under heavy pooling to about −0.038 as the model approaches the flexible fit. Report the path with alpha_sensitivity() rather than fixing a value.

Inference

Turning cell effects into the estimand you actually report, without losing the uncertainty about the grouping.

aggregate()

aggregate(x, type = c("overall", "dynamic", "group", "calendar"), weights = NULL, level = 0.95, ...)

The cohort-time effects are rarely the final object. Overall ATT, event-study profiles, and by-cohort effects are all linear aggregators of the cell effects, so the specification question is orthogonal to the choice of aggregator.

For a bayes_ph posterior the aggregator is applied to each draw and summarised across the chain — which is the whole point of the Bayesian route, since the reported interval then propagates uncertainty about the partition itself. For an l0_ph or ph_fit object it is computed from the fitted effects with the plug-in variance w' R S⁻¹ R' w, conditioning on the selected partition being correct.

coclustering()

coclustering(x)

The K × K posterior similarity matrix: the probability that cells j and k land in the same group. This is the honest summary of the grouping structure. A single partition states that certain cells are equal; the co-clustering matrix says how firmly the data support each such statement.

A diffuse matrix is not a failure of the method. With few, correlated cells it is the correct report that the fine grouping is genuinely uncertain.

point_partition()

point_partition(x, loss = c("VI", "binder"))

The posterior mean effects average over partitions and so match no single grouping. When one grouping must be reported, it should be chosen by minimising a loss against the whole posterior, not by taking the most-visited partition, which is unstable.

VI is the variation-of-information loss of Wade and Ghahramani, information-theoretic and less prone to returning too many small clusters. Binder counts disagreeing pairs weighted by their co-clustering probability. Candidates are the partitions the sampler actually visited plus every cut of a hierarchical clustering of 1 − Π̂, which supplies well-formed candidates the chain may never have hit exactly.

Returns an integer vector with loss and n_groups attributes.

confint(), coef(), vcov()

Standard extractors. confint() on a bayes_ph object returns posterior percentiles; on a ph_fit it returns Wald intervals from the plug-in variance. vcov() is defined for ph_fit objects and returns the K × K grouped covariance with cell labels attached.

Specification testing

The guard against reading a partition of noise as structure.

homogeneity_test()

homogeneity_test(object, pre = NULL, nsim = 10000L, center = TRUE, seed = NULL)

Ask this before grouping anything. In the paper's second application the ℓ0 estimator splits 138 event effects into five neat bands and the DP estimator reports between 2.7 and 7.6 groups — while a randomization test cannot reject a single common effect. Below a signal-to-noise ratio of one, such groups are quantiles of noise.

Provenance

Only assessment 3 is the paper's procedure. Assessments 1 and 2 were added for this package, principally because assessment 3 needs placebo estimates that many designs cannot supply — including the paper's own first application, whose 2004 cohort is treated from the first period of the window and so has no pre-treatment cells at all. The printed output carries the same tags.

1. Common-effect test package addition

This test is not in the paper. The paper proposes no omnibus test of the common-effect null; its only reported p-value is the randomization p of assessment 3.

It is nonetheless implied by the paper's own two-stage Gaussian likelihood (eq. 41). Under the null that all K effects share a common value, the GLS deviance of the fully pooled fit is χ² on K − 1 degrees of freedom — the generalised Cochran Q statistic with a full covariance rather than independent variances. It uses the exact cross-cell covariance, which is what makes it trustworthy where a test built on independent standard errors would not be, and it is reported for every design.

2. Excess-dispersion heterogeneity share adapted

The max{0, ·} decomposition and the heterogeneity share are the paper's, from §5.2.3. What is adapted is the noise gauge: the paper estimates it from placebo estimates as s²pre, whereas this block estimates it from the first-stage covariance, so the decomposition is available on designs with no placebo. With M the centring projection, the dispersion expected under a common effect is tr(M Sigma M)/(K-1), and the excess over that is the genuine component.

The descriptive ratio sd(tau)/median(se) reported alongside it is the paper's, from §5.1.

3. Placebo gauge and randomization test the paper's, §5.2.3

Supplied only when you pass pre — a pre-treatment summary per cell, constructed symmetrically with the post-treatment one. Under no-anticipation it is a direct internal estimate of sampling noise. The randomization test then exchanges each cell's pre- and post-treatment summaries; because the exchange is internal to a cell, it holds the cross-cell dependence structure fixed by construction, which is what makes it valid when cells share controls.

Two details here are the package's rather than the paper's: the p-value carries the usual (1 + #{ref ≥ obs})/(nsim + 1) Monte Carlo correction, which keeps it strictly positive; and the summaries are centred before exchanging.

Why center = TRUE is the default package modification

Under the null the effects share a common value, not a zero one. The post-treatment summaries are therefore shifted by that common effect while the pre-treatment ones are not, and exchanging them unshifted injects that shift into the reference distribution, making the test conservative. Centring removes the shift and leaves the deviations exchangeable, which is the null actually being tested.

It matters only when the common effect is large relative to the noise. At the scale of the paper's second application the two conventions agree to three decimals, so nothing published turns on it. Set center = FALSE for the literal unshifted exchange.

An open reconciliation

Run as described above on the paper's reported inputs (spre = 0.0065, spost = 0.0050), this implementation returns p = 0.998, not the 0.71 the paper reports. The mechanism is arithmetic: a 50/50 exchange gives reference draws with dispersion √(½s²pre + ½s²post) ≈ 0.0058, so the observed 0.0050 sits below almost every draw. Reaching 0.71 would need a ratio near 1.06 rather than the reported 1.30.

The substantive conclusion is unaffected — 0.998 fails to reject a common effect even more decisively than 0.71 does — but the exact statistic, exchange scheme, or tail convention behind the published number is not yet pinned down. Treat the reported p as the paper's and this one as the package's until that is settled.

Diagnostics

Four ways to check that the numbers you are about to report are the numbers the model implies.

enumerate_partitions()

enumerate_partitions(object, alpha = 1, mu0 = NULL, sigma0_sq = NULL, max_cells = 11L)

When K is small, the posterior over partitions can be computed in closed form rather than sampled: evaluate the collapsed marginal likelihood and the CRP prior at every one of the B_K partitions and normalise. With seven cells that is 877 partitions.

Use it to validate a sampler run, or simply to report the exact posterior instead of an approximate one. Bell numbers grow faster than exponentially, so this is infeasible beyond about eleven cells — B₁₂ is over four million.

ex <- enumerate_partitions(d, alpha = 1)

#>   Overall effect        : -0.0240  [-0.0477, -0.0003]
#>
#>   Most probable partitions:
#>     0.227  {2004:2004,2004:2005,2004:2007,2006:2006,2006:2007,2007:2007} {2004:2006}

The aggregate posterior is a finite mixture of normals — one component per partition — and its quantiles come from inverting the mixture CDF, so they are exact rather than simulated.

ph_rhat()

ph_rhat(x, split = TRUE)

Gelman–Rubin statistics and effective sample sizes for the two scalar summaries that matter: the overall aggregate effect and the number of groups. Needs chains ≥ 2; with one chain only the effective sample sizes are reported. split halves each chain first, which catches within-chain trends the classic statistic can miss.

fit <- bayes_ph(d, chains = 4, iters = 3000, burn = 500)
ph_rhat(fit)

#>  number of groups 1.000 5075  2.2027 0.6113

alpha_sensitivity() and lambda_sensitivity()

alpha_sensitivity(object, alpha_grid = c(0.1, 0.25, 0.5, 1, 2, 5, 10, 25, 50, 100), type = "overall", level = 0.95, ..., seed = NULL) lambda_sensitivity(object, lambda_grid = NULL, type = "overall", by = c("lambda", "m"), level = 0.95, n_bic = NULL, ...)

Both estimators have one knob that slides them between full pooling and the fully flexible fit — the concentration alpha for the DP model, the penalty lambda for ℓ0. These trace what the knob does.

Aggregate or per cell

With type = "overall" each row is one tuning value and the estimand is the overall ATT. With type = "cells" the path is reported for every cohort-time effect separately, one row per (tuning value, cell).

The per-cell view is usually the more informative one, and for a specific reason: the aggregate is famously robust to over-pooling, so a flat overall path can hide cells that move a great deal. On the Callaway–Sant'Anna data the overall effect barely shifts across most of the range, while the individual effects travel from the pooled value to their flexible estimates:

Read right to left, that is the agglomeration in reverse: at m = 7 the values are the seven flexible estimates, and each step merges one more pair until everything meets at the pooled value. Lines that converge are the cells the method is merging, and where they meet is the penalty at which it does so.

Some group counts are unreachable by any lambda

Indexing by lambda and by m are genuinely different paths, and the first can skip values the second visits. The stopping rule merges while a pair's cost falls below lambda × |A| × |B|, and that factor grows as groups absorb one another, so the effective per-merge threshold is not monotone in the merge order. When a later merge is cheaper in threshold terms than an earlier one, raising lambda past the earlier merge triggers both at once.

On the paper's first application this is not hypothetical: the thresholds run 0.079, 0.686, 0.397, 1.350, 2.765, 3.571, so no value of lambda selects five groups. Pass by = "m" to walk the path one merge at a time and visit every count from K down to 1.

One asymmetry worth noting when reading the two paths side by side: the ℓ0 bands narrow as the penalty tightens, because they condition on the selected partition being correct. The Bayesian bands do not, because they average over partitions.

Reporting the path rather than a single value is the paper's own recommendation. Choosing the tuning parameter from the data turns it into a data-dependent object, with the usual empirical-Bayes consequences — understated posterior uncertainty and a double use of the data.

On this design the shortcut reproduces the aggregate to three decimals but moves an individual co-clustering probability by 0.70 — which is why "exact" is the default.

Simulation

A calibrated partial-homogeneity DGP, and the Monte Carlo that maps the separation boundary.

ph_design()

ph_design(N = 2000L, T = 10L, cohorts = c(0, 3, 5, 7), sigma = 1)

Sets up a balanced staggered panel. The default is the paper's headline design: 2000 units, 10 periods, cohorts entering at 3, 5 and 7, giving K = 18 post-treatment cells and a mean flexible standard error of 0.0544. N must divide evenly among the cohorts; 0 marks the never-treated group.

The design is built once and reused across replications, since only the errors vary.

ph_truth()

ph_truth(design, m_star, delta)

Partitions the cells into m_star near-equal groups and puts the group means on an evenly spaced grid with adjacent gap delta * sd(tau_flex). The unit-free delta is the whole story: it is the distance between adjacent levels in standard errors of the flexible estimates.

The paper sweeps m_star over 1, 3, 6, 9, 18 and delta over 3, 6, 12, with 6 as the headline.

ph_sample()

ph_sample(design, tau_true)

Draws one replication and returns it as an ordinary ph_data object, so every estimator in the package applies to it directly. Accepts either a numeric vector or a ph_truth() list.

sim_study()

sim_study(design, m_star, delta, R = 100L, methods = c("pooled", "flexible", "oracle", "l0", "bayes"), level = 0.95, bayes_args = list(iters = 600L, burn = 200L), l0_args = list(), seed = NULL, progress = interactive())

Runs the Monte Carlo and returns one row per estimator with variance relative to flexible, absolute bias, RMSE, recovered group count, adjusted Rand index against the true partition, and coverage and length for both the cohort-time effects and the overall ATT.

The oracle is handed the true partition and is infeasible — it is the efficiency floor the feasible estimators are chasing.

Cost

The Bayesian estimator dominates the run time, since every replication runs a full chain. The paper uses 500 replications; the defaults here are much smaller so an example finishes quickly. Reproducing a published row takes hours, not seconds.

Supporting function

adjusted_rand(a, b)

Adjusted Rand index between two partitions — 1 for identical groupings (relabelling invariant), 0 in expectation for independent ones. Used to score recovery.

Choices that move your results

Three defaults the theory does not force. All are exposed rather than buried, because each one changes the answer.

1. The BIC sample size on the two-stage route

The deviance form of BIC needs a sample size for its penalty, and the answer is not forced: the K first-stage estimates are the observations, while the asymptotics justifying Schwarz's rate run in the number of units.

The default is n_bic = K, which is what the paper's replication code uses and what reproduces its reported group counts. On mpdta, n_bic = K selects four groups; n_bic = 500 (the counties) selects three. Report which you used.

2. The data-scaled base measure

The defaults mu0 = median(tau) and sigma0_sq = (10 * sd(tau))^2 match the paper's applications and keep the prior diffuse on whatever scale the outcome happens to have. They are also mildly empirical-Bayes, since the prior then depends on the data. Supply fixed values for a prior that is genuinely independent of it.

3. Exact versus diagonal assignment moves

The published replication scripts use the diagonal shortcut in the cluster-assignment moves and the exact covariance only in the recorded draws. The paper states in two places that the exact covariance is used throughout. This package defaults to marginal = "exact", matching what the paper reports, and keeps "diagonal" available to reproduce the scripts.

On the mpdta design the choice moves an individual co-clustering probability by 0.70 and the posterior expected group count by 0.44, while leaving the aggregate essentially unchanged. If you report co-clustering probabilities, this is not a free choice.

The test suite asserts identities rather than stored numbers, so a failure means the estimator is wrong rather than merely different:

  • the singleton partition returns the first stage unchanged, with zero deviance;
  • pooling in a balanced orthogonal design divides each cell's variance by its group size;
  • the grouped fit satisfies the exact grouped normal equations under the full covariance;
  • the micro-panel and two-stage routes agree exactly, as sufficiency requires;
  • the CRP prior sums to one over all B_K partitions, for several K and alpha;
  • the Gibbs sampler agrees with exact enumeration;
  • the agglomeration path is nested and its deviance monotone;
  • the common-effect test holds its nominal size under a simulated common effect.

R CMD check passes with no errors, warnings or notes.

Limits and open questions

What the package does not do, and what is still unsettled.

Performance

The Gibbs sampler is pure R and costs roughly O(iters × K × m³). This is comfortable at K = 18 and fine at K = 7. At K in the hundreds — the stacked-event-study case — it will be slow, and an Rcpp backend is the fix. That was deliberately deferred so that version 0.1 does not force a compiler on every user.

Search is still a heuristic

Exact minimisation over partitions requires searching all B_K of them. The greedy agglomerative search is O(K³) but carries no approximation guarantee; search = "exact-cost" removes the orthogonality approximation from the merge costs but remains greedy. For genuinely small K, enumerate_partitions() sidesteps the issue entirely by computing the exact posterior.

Inference after selection

The l0_ph plug-in intervals are valid conditional on the selected partition and can under-cover unconditionally, as with inference after any model-selection step. The package prints this caveat on every such fit. A selective-inference correction that conditions on the selection event is an open problem; the Bayesian posterior sidesteps it by propagating partition uncertainty instead, which is why it is the recommended route.

Not yet implemented

The stacked-design application of the paper's Section 5.2 is not wired up as a worked example, since it depends on an external replication package. The machinery is present — pass the event-level effects and their pre-treatment counterparts to homogeneity_test(d, pre = ...).

Covariate-dependent partition priors, which would let observable characteristics inform the grouping itself, are a substantial extension the paper flags as future work and are not implemented.

Heterogeneous errors

The theory extends to a general error covariance, entering everywhere through Omega^-1, and the two-stage route already accommodates this by accepting whatever joint covariance the first stage produces. The block-specific variance sampler for an unknown heterogeneous covariance is described in the paper's Appendix C but is not exposed as a user-facing option in this version.
